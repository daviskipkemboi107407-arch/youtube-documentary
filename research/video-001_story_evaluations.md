## 01. The Fast Radio Burst That Would Not Repeat

**Working title:** *The Fast Radio Burst That Would Not Repeat*
**Central question:** What is producing the one-off fast radio bursts we cannot localize — and is it categorically different from the repeating sources we now can?
**Pitch:** FRBs were a one-line mystery for a decade. Then CHIME/CHIME/FRB and ASKAP localization pinned some bursts to specific dwarf galaxies and to magnetars in our own Milky Way. The interesting story is now the *split*: repeaters vs. apparent one-offs. The honest question is whether the categories are real or a selection effect.
**Why compelling:** real-time discovery story with named instruments, named scientists, named papers. Tension between competing explanations (magnetars, compact-object mergers, exotic cosmology) is unresolved in a productive way.
**Evidence base:** strong — CHIME catalog, ASKAP localizations, foundational papers (Lorimer 2007, Petroff et al., CHIME/FRB Collaboration papers). Open-access summaries available.
**Visual potential:** strong — radio telescope imagery, sky maps, time-frequency "waterfall" plots (legitimately published), magnetar illustrations, dwarf-galaxy photos. (Generated visuals must not invent evidence.)
**Originality angle:** focus on the repeater vs. non-repeater *split* and what localization has ruled out — most existing docs are pre-2020 and treat FRBs as a single mystery.
**15–25 minute fit:** yes — three-act structure: discovery → localization → current debate. ~18–22 minutes.
**Risks:** the science has moved fast; old docs are visibly outdated. The script must cite current sources (2020–2025). Some terminology dense; needs careful narration.
**Production feasibility:** high — no exotic footage required; public-domain imagery and diagrams cover it.
**Scores:** C1 9, C2 9, C3 9, C4 8, C5 8, C6 8, C7 8, C8 8 = **72** (exceptional)

## 02. The Cell That Survived 28,000 Years

**Working title:** *The Cell That Survived 28,000 Years*
**Central question:** What does it actually mean to "revive" a Pleistocene organism — and how do we know the revived cells are not modern contaminants?
**Pitch:** 2020s research on permafrost-revival of bdelloid rotifers and certain plant material raised a real scientific question: where is the line between dormant-but-viable and revived? The honest story is *not* "ancient beast comes back to life" — it's about cryptobiosis, glass transition temperatures, and contamination controls.
**Why compelling:** the clickbait version ("ancient worm lives!") is wrong and there's a clean opportunity to *correct* it with the actual science. The viewer leaves smarter.
**Evidence base:** strong — published primary literature (e.g., Shmakova et al. 2021 on bdelloid rotifers; related tardigrade work). Cryobiology and permafrost microbiology is a real field.
**Visual potential:** strong — microscopy footage, permafrost core imagery, time-lapse of rehydration, diagrams of vitrification.
**Originality angle:** the documentary angle is "what does revival mean scientifically" — not the tabloid angle.
**15–25 minute fit:** yes — clean three-act: permafrost → cryptobiosis → what 'revived' actually means.
**Risks:** the topic can feel narrow; needs a strong narrator to carry the biophysics.

## 03. The Microbe That Eats Metal

**Working title:** *The Microbe That Eats Metal*
**Central question:** How can a living cell transfer electrons to a solid mineral — and what does that tell us about the boundary between geochemistry and biology?
**Pitch:** Shewanella and Geobacter species perform extracellular electron transport (EET). They literally breathe solid iron and manganese oxides. The story has two layers: the basic science (how the cell wiring works), and the implication (early life on Earth may have looked like this; microbial fuel cells, biogeochemistry).
**Why compelling:** genuinely counter-intuitive; "electrical bacteria" reads like science fiction but is established lab science. Animations of cytochrome conduits work well visually.
**Evidence base:** strong — decades of geomicrobiology, NASA/astrobiology relevance (used in subsurface life detection frameworks).
**Visual potential:** strong — SEM imagery of nanowires (legitimately published), biofilm footage, clean animation surface for the molecular machinery.
**Originality angle:** astrobiology tie-in: if we find life on Mars or Europa, the detection method is partly EET-style. Few channels have framed it this way.
**15–25 minute fit:** yes — three-act: discovery of EET → molecular mechanism → implications.
**Risks:** the molecular detail can become dense; needs careful pacing.
